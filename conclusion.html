Where our ideas meet

Conclusion

Throughout our readings, we have explored how consciousness is shaped by experience, interactions with others, and the world around us. Initially, we thought that deliberation simply let a maker imagine another’s suffering, compare designs, and revise before consequences are felt, with the finished product preserving only the chosen response. Yet we discovered that Scarry never narrates this reasoning, and skilled habit could produce relief without comparison. Her verdict that a coat works “more successfully” than an embrace, however, showed deliberation surviving within inherited design: compassion directs attention toward suffering, deliberation assesses the means of relief, and imagination gives that assessment material form. Gell’s kolam, frustrating the demon it confronts, refines this standard: deliberation seeks relief for a protected party, rather than the object’s user.

The difference between a chair and a rack further develops this idea. A maker can build both from the same knowledge of what a body can bear. However, Scarry’s chair is shaped like “perceived-pain-wished-gone” (290). We found that the wish, the felt quality of pain, decides between them. The chair holds “the structure of a perception” (289) without feeling anything. This means phenomenality cannot be reduced to structure. But who is doing the wishing? The maker usually does not feel the pain she is answering; she senses it in someone else. Reading Scarry next to Hegel, we concluded that phenomenality is felt by one person but completed between persons through the things they make.

If phenomenality is completed through our interactions with others and the things we make, then consciousness may also be understood as something continually developing through experience. Across our readings of Scarry and Montaigne, we see consciousness as an evolving process rather than something fixed or independent of experience. Scarry demonstrates how reciprocal interactions with artifacts transform our sentient states, while Montaigne emphasizes that the self is continually changing alongside those experiences. Together, their arguments suggest that narration emerges through the connections between successive experiences, as each state influences what follows. Although the self never remains entirely the same, these connections create continuity and direction, allowing our experiences to continually reshape our values, beliefs, and understanding of the world.

Our understanding of consciousness also raises questions about how objects shape what we experience. For Scarry, feeling originates in the person, so a chair takes its shape from “perceived-pain-wished-gone” and a telescope extends vision (Scarry 285, 290). The object answers the feeling. Gell reverses this, since a complex pattern traps its viewer in a “pleasurable frustration” that the viewer did not have before (Gell 80). If both are right, then what it feels like to be us depends on objects outside the body, and a purely neural account leaves something out. Perhaps objects only shape what we feel, or perhaps they are part of what is doing the feeling.

When a coatmaker’s coat warms a stranger, consider who chose that warmth. The stranger meets that warmth as continuity, a change that arrives unnoticed. We argue that Scarry's made objects serve as “materializations of the elusive embodied capacity for memory” (Scarry 283), so that change can travel through something that someone else selected before it gets to the receiver; Reconsolidation names a selection that is carried across different people, chosen by the maker and absorbed by the receiver. Gell complicates this idea. His patterns keep the receiver's part forever unfinished, so the selection never wholly belongs to the person it changes. If the maker's aim enters the causal stream at the making, then consciousness might influence its course.

As we considered how experience shapes consciousness, we also returned to the question of free will. We have come to see that, to a degree, consciousness does more than register the world. It helps determine what we make of it and what we do next. Scarry shows that felt experience gives creation its direction. Discomfort becomes a wish for relief, and an artifact carries that wish into the world before changing another person's experience in return. Plato adds that an unfamiliar experience can unsettle what someone takes to be real and gradually redirect their choices. These readings suggest that phenomenality connects what is felt to what becomes possible. Insofar as this holds, consciousness involves more than the physical processing of information. Our ability to reflect on experiences, consider alternatives, and reshape our understanding of ourselves reveals how consciousness continually develops through our interactions with the world. Dennett's “multiple drafts” model might describe how the brain processes information, but it does not fully account for what it feels like to experience, interpret, and influence the world around us.
